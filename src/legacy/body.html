What it is

Quantum physics, quantum technology and quantum computing are three different things

People use the words as if they meant the same thing. Separating them makes everything else on this page easier to follow.

Quantum physics

The rules that govern matter and light at the scale of atoms and below. Energy comes in discrete packets called quanta. Particles also behave like waves. A system can be in a superposition of several states until it is measured, and the result of a measurement is random, with probabilities set by the state.

These rules have been tested for a century. They already underpin transistors, lasers, LEDs, solar cells, MRI scanners and atomic clocks. That is often called the first quantum revolution.

Quantum technology

The second quantum revolution. Instead of using quantum effects as a side effect inside bulk materials, engineers now build and control individual quantum systems on purpose: single atoms, ions, photons, electron spins and superconducting circuits.

Four families result: computing, communication, sensing and simulation. They share the same ingredients (superposition, interference, entanglement) and the same enemy (noise from the environment).

Quantum computing

One branch of quantum technology. A quantum computer stores information in qubits and runs algorithms that steer probability amplitudes so that wrong answers cancel and the right answer is likely to be measured.

It is not a faster laptop. It is a different kind of machine that gives large advantages on some problems, small ones on others, and none on most everyday tasks.

Why it exists

The purpose: do what classical machines cannot do efficiently

Quantum technology is not built for speed in general. It is built for four specific jobs.

1. Simulate nature. In 1981 Richard Feynman pointed out that a classical computer needs exponentially more resources to track a quantum system, so the natural simulator is another quantum system. Molecules, catalysts, magnets and superconductors are the target.

2. Solve certain mathematical problems with fewer steps. Shor’s algorithm factors large integers in polynomial time. Grover’s algorithm searches unstructured data with a quadratic speedup. Other algorithms help with linear algebra, sampling and estimating averages.

3. Measure more precisely. A quantum probe such as an atom or a spin is extremely sensitive to magnetic fields, gravity, rotation and time. Quantum sensors already tell time for satellite navigation and image brain activity.

4. Communicate with physics-backed security. Measuring an unknown quantum state disturbs it, so eavesdropping on a quantum key exchange leaves a detectable trace. Quantum networks will also link quantum computers together.

Why the speedup is real for some problems

The best known classical algorithm for factoring, the general number field sieve, takes time that grows faster than any polynomial in the number of digits. Shor’s algorithm grows roughly as the cube of the number of bits. The chart shows the shape of that gap on a log scale. It ignores constant factors and the large overhead of error correction, so read it as a statement about how the cost grows, not as a prediction of run time.

For unstructured search the gain is smaller. Grover gives a square-root speedup, which turns 10¹² steps into 10⁶ queries but does not make hard problems easy.

Technologies

Four families of quantum technology

Computing gets the headlines, but communication and sensing are further along commercially.

Quantum computing

Research, early cloud

Qubits plus gates plus measurement. Three models exist: gate-based (circuits, the one on this page), annealing (optimisation by slowly changing a Hamiltonian) and analog (a device that directly mimics one physical system).

Today
Hundreds to a few thousand noisy physical qubits, cloud access, first small logical qubits with error correction.
Next
Error-corrected machines with hundreds of logical qubits and billions of operations.

Quantum communication

In use

Quantum key distribution (BB84, E91) shares encryption keys whose secrecy rests on physics. Quantum networks send entangled photons between nodes, and repeaters will extend range beyond the roughly one hundred kilometres loss allows in fibre.

Today
Fibre QKD networks including China’s Beijing to Shanghai backbone, satellite links since the Micius satellite in 2016, and European EuroQCI plans.
Next
Entanglement distribution, a quantum internet, and linking separate quantum processors.

Quantum sensing

Early commercial

A superposition accumulates phase in proportion to a field or to elapsed time. Reading that phase gives extreme sensitivity. Atomic clocks, diamond NV magnetometers, atom interferometers and squeezed-light detectors all work this way.

Today
Atomic clocks in satellite navigation, wearable magnetoencephalography, quantum gravimeters in field trials, squeezed light in gravitational-wave detectors.
Next
Navigation without GPS, mineral and water mapping, cheaper medical imaging, entangled sensor arrays.

Quantum simulation

Pilots

Analog simulators use cold atoms in optical lattices, Rydberg atom arrays or trapped ions as a stand-in for a material you cannot compute. Digital simulation uses a gate-based computer to evolve the same model step by step.

Today
Studies of magnetism, phase transitions and dynamics at sizes that stress classical methods.
Next
Chemistry and materials with accuracy that classical approximations cannot reach.

Post-quantum cryptography is not quantum technology

It is classical mathematics, run on ordinary computers, designed to resist attacks from quantum computers. NIST published its first three post-quantum standards in 2024: ML-KEM (FIPS 203) for key exchange, and ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) for signatures. Most organisations will migrate to these long before they ever buy a QKD link, because they run on existing networks and hardware. The two approaches are complementary.

How it is used

A quantum computer is an accelerator inside a classical workflow

Nobody runs a whole application on a quantum chip. A classical program prepares the problem, calls the quantum processor for the one step it does well, and processes the answer.

Find the structureDecide whether the problem has the hidden structure a quantum algorithm exploits, such as periodicity, a Hamiltonian, or a searchable oracle.
EncodeMap the problem onto qubits: a molecule becomes a Hamiltonian of Pauli operators, a search becomes an oracle, a graph becomes a cost function.
CompileBreak the circuit into the device’s native gates, route it across the qubit connectivity, and shorten it as much as possible.
Run in shotsExecute the circuit thousands of times. Each shot ends in a random measurement, and the distribution holds the answer.
Mitigate or correctReduce the damage from noise with error mitigation today, and with error-correcting codes on future machines.
Post-processClassical code turns the counts into the final result, for example continued fractions in Shor’s algorithm or an optimiser update in VQE.

How you can use one today

Several vendors offer cloud access to real quantum processors, including IBM Quantum, Amazon Braket, Azure Quantum and the cloud services of ion-trap, neutral-atom and photonic companies. Free tiers or credits exist on some of them, so check current terms. All the major SDKs also ship simulators that run on a laptop.

A sensible path is to write circuits for 2 to 5 qubits, run them on a simulator first, then run the same circuit on real hardware and compare the two histograms. The difference between them is a direct picture of noise.

Quantum sensors and QKD systems are bought as instruments. They plug into existing navigation, imaging or network equipment and need no quantum programming.

Hardware

Eight ways to build a qubit

No platform has won. Each trades speed, fidelity, connectivity and manufacturability differently, and the field keeps changing, so treat the company names as examples rather than a ranking.

Superconducting circuits

A small circuit with a Josephson junction behaves as an artificial atom. Chips are cooled to about 10 to 20 millikelvin and driven with microwave pulses.

Strengths
Gates in tens to hundreds of nanoseconds, chip-industry fabrication, large software ecosystem.
Challenges
Short coherence (tens to hundreds of microseconds), fixed neighbour connectivity, dense wiring, dilution refrigerators.
Examples
IBM, Google, Rigetti, IQM, Alice & Bob.

Trapped ions

Individual charged atoms are held in electric fields and laser-cooled. Their internal energy levels are the qubit, and lasers or microwaves drive the gates.

Strengths
Among the highest gate fidelities, coherence of seconds or more, all-to-all connectivity within a trap.
Challenges
Slower gates (microseconds to milliseconds), growing the number of ions per trap, precise optics.
Examples
Quantinuum, IonQ, Alpine Quantum Technologies.

Neutral atoms

Atoms such as rubidium or strontium are held in arrays by focused laser beams called optical tweezers. Exciting them to Rydberg states makes neighbours interact and entangle.

Strengths
Hundreds to thousands of qubits, reconfigurable geometry, no cryostat, natural for analog simulation.
Challenges
Atom loss, slower cycle times, continuous reloading and mid-circuit readout.
Examples
QuEra, Pasqal, Atom Computing, Infleqtion.

Photonic

Photons carry the qubit in their path, polarisation, time bin or in squeezed modes of light. Circuits are waveguides on a chip with beam splitters and phase shifters.

Strengths
Operates largely at room temperature, natural for networking, compatible with chip manufacturing.
Challenges
Photon loss, probabilistic two-photon gates, enormous component counts, fast cryogenic detectors.
Examples
PsiQuantum, Xanadu, Quandela.

Silicon spin qubits

The spin of a single electron, or of a donor nucleus, is trapped in a transistor-like structure and controlled with microwaves and voltages.

Strengths
Nanometre size, long coherence in purified silicon, direct path to existing CMOS factories.
Challenges
Device-to-device variability, wiring density, reaching high fidelity across many qubits.
Examples
Intel, Diraq, Quantum Motion, Silicon Quantum Computing.

Diamond colour centres

A nitrogen atom next to a missing carbon atom (an NV centre) has a spin that can be initialised and read with light, even at room temperature.

Strengths
Room-temperature operation, atom-scale sensor size, optical readout.
Challenges
Hard to scale into a computer. Mainly used for sensing and as network nodes.
Uses
Magnetometry, nanoscale NMR, quantum memory.

Quantum annealers

Superconducting flux qubits implement an Ising-type Hamiltonian that is slowly changed so the system settles into a low-energy solution of an optimisation problem.

Strengths
Thousands of qubits available today for a specific problem class.
Challenges
Not a universal gate-model computer. Whether it beats the best classical heuristics on practical problems is still debated.
Examples
D-Wave.

Topological qubits

An idea in which information lives in non-local properties of exotic quasiparticles, so local noise cannot easily damage it.

Strengths
If realised, built-in protection could greatly reduce error-correction overhead.
Challenges
Microsoft reported a topological device in 2025, and the physics behind that claim is still being examined by the community.
Status
Early research.
Where it is used

Applications, and how mature each one really is

The tag on each card says how close the application is to everyday use. Several well-known promises are still research.

Drug discovery and chemistry

Research

Molecular energies are quantum problems. Accurate simulation of reaction mechanisms, binding and enzymes is limited by classical approximations. A widely studied target is the iron-molybdenum cofactor of nitrogenase, which fixes nitrogen in nature.

How
Phase estimation on an error-corrected machine, or VQE on small molecules today.
Reality check
Useful advantage is expected to need fault-tolerant hardware. Today’s demonstrations use small molecules.

Materials and batteries

Research

Electrolytes, catalysts, magnets and high-temperature superconductors are hard because electrons interact strongly. Simulating them could guide the search for better batteries and cheaper catalysts.

How
Analog simulators for model Hamiltonians now, digital simulation later.
Reality check
Analog simulators already study idealised models. Real-material accuracy needs larger machines.

Cryptography and security

In use

A large fault-tolerant machine running Shor’s algorithm would break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. Data stolen today can be decrypted later, so migration is already under way.

How
Post-quantum algorithms on classical hardware. QKD in a few high-security links.
Reality check
NIST plans to deprecate RSA and ECC after 2030 and disallow them from 2035.

Optimisation and logistics

Pilots

Routing, scheduling, portfolio selection and supply chains are combinatorial. Annealers and QAOA are tried on them by airlines, ports, banks and manufacturers.

How
Encode as an Ising or QUBO problem and sample low-energy solutions.
Reality check
Clear advantage over strong classical solvers has not been shown for practical sizes.

Finance

Research

Pricing derivatives and measuring risk rely on Monte Carlo sampling. Amplitude estimation offers a quadratic reduction in the number of samples needed for a given accuracy.

How
Amplitude estimation, quantum linear algebra, portfolio optimisation.
Reality check
The quadratic gain can be eaten by error-correction overhead and the cost of loading data.

AI and machine learning

Research

Ideas include quantum kernels, variational classifiers and faster sampling for generative models. Quantum data from sensors and simulators is a more natural fit than classical images or text.

How
Parameterised circuits trained with classical optimisers.
Reality check
Loading large classical datasets into qubits is a bottleneck, and many proposed speedups have been matched by classical methods.

Energy and climate

Research

Fertiliser production by the Haber–Bosch process consumes on the order of one to two percent of the world’s energy. Better catalysts, carbon-capture materials and fusion plasma models are all quantum-simulation targets.

How
Quantum chemistry and materials simulation, grid optimisation.
Reality check
Long-term. Results depend on fault-tolerant machines.

Timing and navigation

In use

Atomic clocks keep satellite navigation and telecom networks synchronised. Optical clocks are far more precise, and cold-atom accelerometers and gyroscopes can navigate when GPS is unavailable.

How
Ramsey interferometry on atoms, laser-cooled atom interferometers.
Reality check
Clocks are mature. Atom-based inertial navigation is in field trials.

Medical and brain imaging

Early commercial

Optically pumped magnetometers sit in a wearable helmet and record the tiny magnetic fields of brain activity without a bulky cryogenic scanner. Diamond sensors probe single cells and molecules.

How
Spin-based magnetometry.
Reality check
Wearable magnetoencephalography is in research and early clinical use. MRI is built on nuclear spins but is run classically.

Telecom and the quantum internet

Early commercial

Quantum links will secure key exchange and eventually connect quantum computers into larger distributed machines. Repeaters with quantum memories are the key missing component.

How
Entangled photons over fibre or satellite, entanglement swapping and teleportation.
Reality check
QKD links are deployed. A full entanglement-based internet is a research programme.

The future

Where quantum technology came from, and where it is heading

The history shows how slowly foundations turn into products. The roadmap shows what hardware companies and standards bodies are planning.

How we got here

  1. 1900
    Planck introduces the quantumEnergy is exchanged in discrete packets, which solves the black-body problem.
  2. 1925–27
    Quantum mechanics is formulatedHeisenberg, Schrödinger, Born and others set out the rules still used today.
  3. 1947–60
    The first quantum revolutionThe transistor (1947) and the laser (1960) turn quantum theory into industry.
  4. 1981
    Feynman proposes quantum simulationNature is quantum, so simulate it with a quantum machine.
  5. 1984
    BB84 quantum key distributionBennett and Brassard show that physics can protect a key exchange.
  6. 1994–96
    Shor, error correction, GroverFactoring in polynomial time, the first quantum error-correcting codes, and quadratic search.
  7. 2019
    First beyond-classical claimGoogle reports a random-circuit sampling task on 53 superconducting qubits. Classical researchers later narrowed the gap.
  8. 2022
    Nobel Prize in PhysicsAspect, Clauser and Zeilinger are recognised for experiments with entangled photons that established the violation of Bell inequalities.
  9. 2024
    Error correction below threshold, and PQC standardsGoogle reports a surface-code memory whose logical error falls as the code grows. NIST publishes FIPS 203, 204 and 205.

What is planned next

  1. 2026
    Modular, error-corrected prototypesIBM’s roadmap lists Kookaburra, its first module for storing and processing encoded information. In July 2026 IBM and the University of Chicago announced a 70-logical-qubit sampling experiment they describe as beyond classical simulation. That is a company claim in a preprint, so independent checks are pending.
  2. 2027
    Linked modulesIBM’s Cockatoo is planned to join two Kookaburra modules with long-range couplers.
  3. 2029
    First large fault-tolerant machine, by planIBM targets Starling: 200 logical qubits and 100 million operations. Other companies publish their own targets, and these are plans, not guarantees.
  4. 2030
    Start of the RSA and ECC phase-outUnder NIST guidance, quantum-vulnerable algorithms are deprecated after 2030.
  5. 2030s
    Larger logical machinesIBM’s Blue Jay is described with 2,000 logical qubits and a billion operations, without a firm date in its announcement. This is the scale where chemistry and materials results become plausible.
  6. 2035
    RSA and ECC disallowed in NIST guidanceSystems that need long-term secrecy have to migrate well before then.
Read dates carefully. Hardware dates are company targets and have slipped before. Standards dates are policy. Predictions about when a machine will break RSA-2048 vary widely, and published resource estimates have fallen over time, for example to under one million noisy physical qubits in a 2025 analysis by Craig Gidney.

Where it may be used in the future

Designing medicines

Accurate simulation of how a drug binds to a protein, including quantum effects that classical force fields miss, could shorten the search for candidates.

New materials

Room-temperature superconductors, better magnets, lighter and safer batteries and efficient solar absorbers are all searches through quantum-mechanical possibility space.

Cleaner industry

Catalysts for fertiliser, hydrogen and carbon capture could cut a large share of industrial energy use if simulation can guide their design.

Fusion and energy systems

Plasma dynamics and materials for reactor walls are very hard to simulate. Quantum algorithms for differential equations are an active research area.

A quantum internet

Entanglement distribution between cities would allow secure communication, networked sensors with higher precision, and cloud access to quantum computers with private data.

Distributed quantum computing

Linking many smaller processors with photonic interconnects may scale further than a single chip can.

GPS-free navigation

Compact cold-atom inertial sensors could keep ships, aircraft and vehicles on course for hours without satellite signals.

Earth and health sensing

Gravimeters for mapping aquifers and tunnels, and magnetometers that read brain and heart signals without shielded rooms.

What could slow it down

  • Error rates. Algorithms that matter need logical error rates near 10⁻¹² or lower, many orders of magnitude below today’s physical qubits.
  • Overhead. Each logical qubit may need hundreds to thousands of physical qubits, depending on the code and the hardware.
  • Engineering. Wiring, cooling, lasers, real-time decoding and manufacturing yield all have to scale together.
  • Algorithms. Few problems have a proven large speedup. Finding more, and showing they beat the best classical methods, is open research.
  • Data loading and readout. Getting big classical data in, and big answers out, can erase a speedup.
  • People. The field needs engineers who understand both quantum physics and large-scale software and hardware.
Common myths

Six things people get wrong

“It tries every answer at once.”

A register of n qubits holds 2ⁿ amplitudes, but a measurement returns only one n-bit result. Algorithms work by interference that makes the right answer likely, not by reading all branches.

“It will replace my laptop.”

Quantum computers are slow at ordinary tasks and need delicate, expensive infrastructure. They will sit in data centres as accelerators for a few kinds of work.

“It will break all encryption tomorrow.”

Breaking RSA-2048 needs a large fault-tolerant machine that does not exist yet. Symmetric encryption such as AES-256 is only weakened by a square root. Migration to post-quantum standards is under way.

“It speeds up everything.”

Most problems have no known quantum speedup. Many known ones are only quadratic, and that is often not enough to cover the overhead of error correction.

“Entanglement sends messages faster than light.”

Measurement results on each side look random. They only show a correlation once the two sides compare notes by ordinary communication, which is no faster than light.

“More qubits always means better.”

Quality matters as much as count. Gate fidelity, connectivity, coherence, readout and the ability to run error correction decide what a machine can do.

      Rough surface-code estimate in the spirit of the Microsoft resource estimator. Model: threshold 1%, logical error 0.1·(p/0.01)^((d+1)/2), 2(d+1)² physical qubits per logical qubit, 1 µs per round.

        Quantum computing

        BeginnerA classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit can be in a superposition of both, and n qubits hold 2ⁿ amplitudes at once.

        IntermediateQuantum computers use interference: algorithms arrange amplitudes so wrong answers cancel and right answers add. They do not try every answer and pick one.

        AdvancedUseful speedups come from structure (period finding, amplitude amplification, simulation of quantum systems), not from raw parallelism. Noise limits circuit depth today.

        |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, |α|² + |β|² = 1

        Example: 3 qubits = 8 amplitudes; 50 qubits = about 10¹⁵ amplitudes.

        Quantum mechanics

        BeginnerParticles are described by a wavefunction. The Born rule says |ψ|² gives the probability of finding the particle at a place.

        IntermediateThe Schrödinger equation governs how ψ changes in time. Observables are operators; measuring one gives an eigenvalue at random.

        AdvancedThe uncertainty principle comes from non-commuting operators: position and momentum cannot both be sharp.

        iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ, Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2

        Example: An electron in hydrogen occupies orbitals, not orbits.

        Quantum physics

        BeginnerEnergy comes in packets. Light of frequency f carries energy E = hf, which explains the photoelectric effect.

        IntermediateMatter behaves like a wave with wavelength λ = h/p, shown by electron diffraction and the double-slit experiment.

        AdvancedBound systems have discrete energies, such as hydrogen's levels, which produce spectral lines.

        E = hf, λ = h/p, Eₙ = −13.6 eV / n²

        Example: Electrons fired one at a time still build up interference fringes.

        Quantum algorithms

        BeginnerGrover search finds a marked item among N in about √N steps. Shor's algorithm factors integers by finding a hidden period.

        IntermediateQuantum Fourier transform, phase estimation and amplitude amplification are the building blocks. Variational algorithms (VQE, QAOA) mix a quantum circuit with a classical optimiser.

        AdvancedShor runs in polynomial time, about O((log N)³) gates, versus super-polynomial for the best known classical methods. Grover's speedup is only quadratic and is provably optimal for unstructured search.

        k ≈ (π/4)√N, P = sin²((2k+1)θ), sin θ = 1/√N

        Example: Searching 1,000,000 items: about 785 Grover iterations versus about 500,000 classical checks.

        Quantum cryptography

        BeginnerQuantum key distribution (BB84, E91) lets two people share a secret key. Eavesdropping disturbs the photons and is detected.

        IntermediateThe no-cloning theorem forbids copying an unknown quantum state, which is why interception leaves traces. A key is usually abandoned if the error rate exceeds roughly 11%.

        AdvancedShor's algorithm threatens RSA and elliptic-curve encryption, so standards bodies have published post-quantum algorithms (for example NIST's ML-KEM and ML-DSA, 2024) that run on ordinary computers.

        QBER > ~11% ⇒ discard key (BB84, ideal case)

        Example: Banks and governments already pilot QKD links; most organisations are migrating to post-quantum cryptography instead.

        Quantum communication

        BeginnerEntangled pairs let you teleport a quantum state: one Bell pair plus two classical bits move a qubit from Alice to Bob. No matter or faster-than-light signal is sent.

        IntermediatePhotons lose signal in fibre, so long links need quantum repeaters or satellites.

        AdvancedSatellite experiments have distributed entanglement over more than 1,000 km. A quantum internet would link quantum computers and sensors.

        |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2

        Example: Teleportation needs a classical channel, so it cannot send information faster than light.

        Quantum AI

        BeginnerQuantum machine learning uses parameterised circuits whose settings are tuned by a classical optimiser, much like training a neural network.

        IntermediateQuantum kernels map data into a huge quantum feature space. Gradients can be measured with the parameter-shift rule.

        AdvancedOpen problems: loading classical data is costly, and barren plateaus (vanishing gradients) make training hard. Proven advantages are mostly for quantum data.

        ∂⟨O⟩/∂θ = ½[⟨O⟩(θ+π/2) − ⟨O⟩(θ−π/2)]

        Example: Classifying molecular states measured from a quantum sensor.

        Quantum hardware

        BeginnerQubits are built from superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photons or electron spins.

        IntermediateKey numbers: coherence times T₁ and T₂, gate fidelity, qubit connectivity and clock speed.

        AdvancedError correction (for example the surface code) groups many physical qubits into one logical qubit. It works when the physical error rate is below a threshold of roughly 1%.

        p_logical ≈ 0.1·(p/p_th)^((d+1)/2)

        Example: Going from distance d = 3 to d = 5 cuts logical errors again if p is well below threshold.

        Emerging quantum technologies

        BeginnerQuantum sensors use superposition to measure magnetic fields, gravity and time with extreme precision.

        IntermediatePost-quantum cryptography, quantum networks and quantum simulation of chemistry and materials are advancing fast.

        AdvancedThe field is moving from noisy devices toward error-corrected logical qubits; claims of quantum advantage are checked against the best classical algorithms.

        Δφ ∝ 1/N (Heisenberg limit) vs 1/√N (classical)

        Example: Diamond NV-centre sensors image magnetic fields inside living cells.

        FAQ

        Frequently asked questions

        What is a qubit?

        A qubit is the basic unit of quantum information. Unlike a bit, it can be in a superposition of 0 and 1 until it is measured.

        Is quantum computing faster than classical computing for everything?

        No. Quantum computers give large speedups only for specific problems, such as factoring, simulating quantum systems and some search and optimisation tasks. For everyday tasks classical computers remain better.

        Can quantum computers break encryption today?

        Not yet. Breaking RSA-2048 would need a large error-corrected machine that does not exist. Published resource estimates have fallen over the years, which is why organisations are moving to post-quantum cryptography now.

        What is quantum entanglement?

        Entanglement is a correlation between quantum systems that cannot be explained by classical shared information. Measuring one part is correlated with the other, but it cannot be used to send messages faster than light.

        How can I learn quantum computing for free?

        Start with this site's modules and simulations, then try free courses such as IBM Quantum Learning with Qiskit and the Microsoft Learn Azure Quantum path. A little linear algebra helps.

        What is a quantum algorithm?

        A quantum algorithm is a step-by-step procedure for a quantum computer that uses superposition, entanglement and interference to solve a problem, for example Grover's search or Shor's factoring.
